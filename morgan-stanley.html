First, the basics

Wealth management is the part of a bank that looks after money for wealthy clients. Morgan Stanley gives each client a financial advisor, a person who manages their investments and calls them when something needs attention.

The product is the tool those advisors use. Bank software watches every client account. When something notable happens, like a large transfer out or an unusual payment, it creates an alert and sends it to that client's advisor.

The list is where those alerts pile up, waiting to be looked at. The advisor opens each one and decides a single thing: is this worth calling the client about?

That decision is where the money is. A large transfer out can mean a client is moving to another bank. Spot it the same week and an advisor can call and keep the business. Spot it a month later and it has gone. Every alert sitting unread is a client conversation that never happened.

01
Overview

Advisors had a tool they worked around instead of working with

Morgan Stanley is a bank that operates in more than 40 countries. About 80,000 staff look after money for roughly 3.5 million clients. Its software watches those client accounts. When something notable happens, like a large transfer out or an unusual payment, the software creates an alert and sends it to that client's advisor. The advisor then decides one thing: is this worth calling the client about?

The software was creating the right alerts. The problem was what happened next. Advisors got through the newest ones and left the rest sitting. Nobody wrote down the thinking behind their decisions. And the bank had no way to find out which alerts were useful.

I joined as Product Designer to redesign that whole flow: the alerts tool itself, the dashboard that summarises it, the charts advisors read before deciding, and the design system that holds it together. The work ran in two parts. First a three-month research and planning phase. Then design and build across two releases, working one sprint ahead of the engineers, with 2 product managers and 15 engineers.

02
Problem

Checking one alert cost too much, so advisors checked fewer of them

Checking an alert should be quick. Read what happened. Look at what the client holds. Decide if it needs a call. Write down the decision. In the old tool those four steps lived in four different places, and moving between them was slow enough that advisors handled the easy alerts and left the unclear ones.

What the old tool did

Three problems stacked on top of each other. The alert list and the account details were separate pages, so checking an alert meant leaving the list and losing your place. The account and date filters reset when you moved between pages, so advisors had to pick them again every time, and sometimes ended up comparing the wrong things without noticing. And the transactions only appeared once you clicked into them, so confirming an alert took several clicks instead of one look.

Closing an alert had its own problem. There was one close button for every situation. The system saved that someone had closed the alert, but never why. Notes were a single box that got wiped every time someone typed in it. So when an account moved to a new advisor, months of thinking went with the old one.

The result was a tool advisors opened, worked around, and shut. Visits were short and people came back rarely. They put up with it rather than relied on it.

Problem statement Financial advisors only got through part of their alert list, and left the rest sitting, because checking one alert meant leaving the list, the filters reset every time, and closing an alert saved no reason. So client activity went unchecked, the bank learned nothing about which alerts were worth sending, and use of the tool stopped growing. Both the firm's own analytics and my sessions with advisors showed the same thing.

"I spend more time finding the data I need than actually deciding anything. And when I close one out, that judgment just disappears — nobody knows why I made the call."

— Senior financial advisor, user research session
03
Research

Three kinds of evidence pointed at the same cause

I treated the sitting alerts as a research question before a design one. The easy answer was “there are too many alerts”, which points at sending fewer. The evidence said something else. Checking one alert cost too much, and nothing in the system learned from the checks that did happen.

01
Queue Analytics
Advisors weren't working through the list evenly. Most closed alerts came from the newest part of it, and the number dropped off sharply further down. The alerts that sat longest were the unclear ones, not the unimportant ones. Advisors were sorting by effort instead of importance, which is what people do when checking something is expensive.
02
Advisor Shadowing (n=12)
I sat with 12 advisors while they worked. Every one of them left the tool to check an alert, and most kept a second window open to hold their place. Several kept their own spreadsheets to track what they had already seen. A list you need a second list to use is a list that isn't working.
03
Checking Closed Alerts
I took a sample of closed alerts and tried to work out why each one was closed. For most of them I couldn't. The record held a time and a name and nothing else. The bank had run this alert programme for years without the one piece of information that would tell it which alerts were worth sending.

What the numbers couldn't tell me

The numbers showed where alerts stalled, but not why any single one was closed without action. So I ran a two-week diary study with a small group of advisors. Every time they closed an alert, they wrote one line saying why, in their own words. The answers fell into three groups. The client wasn't interested. The advisor didn't think it was worth raising. Or the alert should never have been sent.

That third group changed the brief. Those weren't decisions about clients. They were bug reports about the alerts themselves, and there was nowhere for them to go. From that point the project stopped being only about speed. It became a question of how everyday work could tell the bank something useful.

04
Options Considered

Three options. I ruled out two.

Option A — Rejected
Let the software close low-value alerts by itself
The fastest way to shrink the pile, and the first thing the business asked about. I ruled it out for two reasons. Compliance would not let software close a client alert without a person deciding. And we had no solid way to score what an alert was worth, because the data that would teach it was exactly the data we weren't collecting. Automating on top of that gap hides misses instead of stopping them.
Option B — Rejected
Just reorder the list and change nothing else
The cheapest option, and a fair one: put the most important alerts at the top. I ruled it out because the research already showed advisors sorted by effort, not by position. Reordering an expensive list only changes which alerts get skipped. It also does nothing about the missing record, so the bank still learns nothing about its own alerts.
Option C — Chosen
Cut the cost of one review, and make every closed alert carry a reason
Two changes in one release. First, put the list and the details on one screen, so checking an alert never costs an advisor their place or their filters. Second, replace the single close button with a short list of options that separates alerts still in play from the different reasons an alert ends. Same one click, but now every outcome says something. Make the everyday task cheaper, and have it produce useful data while it runs.

I tested the idea before we built it

The product managers knew this business far better than I did, and they had a firm view: advisors would reject any menu with more than a few options, because every extra choice costs time on a task they do dozens of times a day. That is a fair worry, and it threatened the exact part of the design that made the list produce data. I agreed with the direction but not with building it on a hunch. So I wrote down three things we were assuming and tested them with advisors inside the firm. Writing them down first meant the team could argue with the results instead of with me.

01
Assumption — Panel Over Page
Does a side panel actually beat a full page? A panel keeps you in place but takes width from a table that already has too little. If advisors couldn't read the list next to it, we would have swapped one problem for another.
02
Assumption — More Specific Statuses
Would advisors accept a list of closing options, or treat it as pointless paperwork? The product managers expected a fight. The whole feedback loop rested on this answer, so it was the one worth testing properly.
03
Assumption — Structured Notes
Would advisors tick the ready-made task labels, or ignore them and just type? Labels are what make an account's history searchable later. But only if people actually use them when they are busy.

What the testing showed, including where we were wrong

01
Finding — The Panel Won, But Position Didn't
The panel beat the full page easily and nobody wanted to go back. But after a few alerts, advisors lost track of where they were. The panel wasn't the problem. It just needed next and previous buttons inside it, so moving on never meant going back to the list to find your place.
02
Finding — The Extra Detail Cost Nothing
Advisors did not resist. Picking an option took about as long as the old single button, because they had already made the decision in their head. The old button just threw it away. What did fail was our wording. The first labels used the firm's internal terms, and advisors picked the wrong one until we rewrote every option in the words they use with each other.
03
Finding — Tags Carried, Text Supplemented
Advisors used the task labels far more than we expected. Ticking is faster than typing, and it made the note feel finished. Typing didn't go away, but its job changed. It went from being the whole note to covering the bits a label couldn't. So we put the labels first and kept the text box underneath.

I pulled the findings together and walked the product managers through them. Two assumptions held. The one about advisors resisting did not, which was the useful result. It meant the feedback loop could ship in the first release instead of waiting. Showing this as evidence rather than opinion kept the conversation friendly, and it made testing the next round an easy sell.

05
Decision & Tradeoff

We gave up some columns to keep the list on screen

The panel takes about a third of the screen when it is open, and the table has fourteen columns. That width has to come from somewhere. We chose to let some columns drop out of sight while the panel is open, rather than squeeze every column until none of them are readable. Engineering pushed back, and fairly: hiding data in a data tool is a hard thing to argue for.

+ Gained
Advisors check an alert without leaving the list. They keep their filters, their scroll position, and their place. Task time dropped 32%, and the alerts that used to sit longest were the ones this fixed.
− Lost
About a third of the columns are hidden while the panel is open. Advisors who work across the full width have to close the panel to see everything. For them, that is a step backwards.
Why accepted
The hidden columns are the ones advisors weren't reading mid-check anyway. We picked them by watching what people actually looked at. Losing a few side columns for the length of one check costs less than losing your place on every single one.

The three things we built

A panel that stays open. Clicking an alert opens the client's history next to the list instead of replacing it. The panel has its own next and previous buttons, so an advisor can work through several alerts without going back to the table. That fix came straight out of testing.

Closing options that save a reason. One close button became a short list that separates alerts still in play from the different reasons an alert ends. Every option is still one click. But now the record says why, and a reason that points at a bad alert rule can be told apart from one that points at a fine rule and a client who said no.

A note history that only grows. Notes used to be one box that got overwritten. Now every note is kept, with a label for what happened, the name of who wrote it, and the time. When an account changes hands, the thinking goes with it instead of disappearing.

06
Wireframes

Concept sketches: the structure, not the real screens

The shipped interface is covered by NDA. These are redrawn concept wireframes made to communicate the design reasoning — deliberately rough, with placeholder content and no real client data, amounts, or production visual design. They show the structural decisions, not the product.

01 — Structure: two pages → one list + side panel

The main change. The list and the details used to be separate pages, so checking an alert meant leaving the list and losing both your place and your filters. Now clicking an alert opens a panel beside it, and the panel has up and down buttons so you can work through the list without going back.

02 — The status menu

Closing an alert was the most common outcome and the least understood. Splitting it by reason costs the advisor nothing, still one click, but it turns a dead record into useful information. “Not an opportunity” in particular is a direct report that the alert rule got it wrong.

03 — Inside the side panel

The panel serves two people: whoever is working the list now, and whoever inherits the account later. That drove three decisions. Notes are only ever added, never overwritten, and always show who wrote them. Tick boxes cover the four most common actions, so they can be counted. And next and previous buttons sit in the header, so writing a note never costs you your place.

04 — Dashboard summary

This module answers one question first: how much is still open. Its tabs reuse the same labels as the main tool instead of inventing new ones, so the dashboard teaches the words an advisor will meet when they open the list.

07
Design Decisions

One question sat behind every screen: could an advisor explain this decision a year later?

One row in the list, explained

The owner's name sits with the status.

When several advisors share coverage, the first question about any alert is who has it. So the name sits directly under the status instead of in its own column. That way it stays visible when the panel hides columns, and an advisor scanning the list can see which alerts are theirs.

Closing reasons are grouped, not listed flat.

The first version put every option in one flat list, and advisors kept picking the wrong one. Splitting the list in two, alerts still in play at the top and reasons for closing below, cut those mistakes sharply. We didn't remove a single option.

Notes: I designed the empty state first

Most alerts have no notes yet, so the empty panel is the screen advisors see most. It is also the one that decides whether anyone uses the feature. Instead of leaving it blank, it says what to do next and keeps the note controls visible underneath, with the save button switched off until there is something to save. The tick-box labels sit above the text box, in the order testing showed people work: say what happened, then type the bit the labels don't cover.

The full panel has the opposite problem. Notes are only ever added, newest at the top, each with a name and a time. A long history then reads as a record instead of a wall of text, and the most recent note is where someone new to the account will look first.

The design system

I built and looked after the shared Figma design system that the list, panel, and dashboard all use. It has reusable parts, clear rules for using them, and notes on which states come from the software and which come from a person. That last distinction matters when the record can be audited. Handover to engineering got noticeably quicker once it was in place, and the shared parts made later features faster to design and faster to review.

Designing a sprint ahead of 15 engineers

The team worked in two-week sprints, so design had to be finished a full sprint before building started, with every detail written down. In practice that meant working out every combination before the sprint opened: each status and owner on a row, the panel empty and full, what happens when loading fails, and what happens when two advisors act on the same alert at once. Anything unclear at standup blocked fifteen people, so the job was making decisions early rather than late.

Where the chart tool and the design disagreed

The dashboards use the platform's existing chart tool, and its standard settings weren't always the clearest way to show the numbers. The best example: to let advisors compare a client's mix against a benchmark, I needed the pairs of bars closer together than the tool normally allows, and changing that was not a small job to estimate. Rather than design around it or hand the team an open-ended task, I brought in an engineer from another team who had solved something similar, and we sized the work together before it went into the build plan.

The same lesson held across the release. The tool's default settings are built to draw charts quickly, not to make them clear. So we checked every chart against what the advisor was actually comparing. Where the default got in the way, we either changed it on purpose or picked a different chart, instead of shipping something that drew neatly and read badly.

Accessibility came first, not at the end

Money charts have an accessibility problem most screens don't. Mix, gain and loss, and risk levels are all normally shown with colour alone. Working with the firm's accessibility team, I gave every chart a second signal besides colour: patterns and textures on the bars, labels written directly on them instead of a key off to the side, and a plain table version of every chart. We set contrast targets at the first sketch rather than fixing them after handover, so the compliance review confirmed things instead of finding problems.

08
Outcome

Advisor productivity +35%. Platform adoption +28%. Account review 40% faster.

What changed

Advisor productivity went up 35%, measured in the firm's own analytics after launch. It came from removing the context switch in the middle of the job. Fewer steps per alert, and no setting up filters and finding your place again after each one.

Adoption went up 28%. The clearest sign wasn't one number but a change in shape. Advisors moved from skimming the newest alerts to working through the whole list. The alerts that used to sit longest, the unclear ones that need context to judge, were the ones that started getting looked at.

Account and portfolio review got 40% faster. The redesigned dashboard and charts let an advisor read a client's position and recent activity in one go, instead of piecing it together from separate pages first.

And the list started producing data. Every closed alert now carries a reason, so the bank can tell the difference between an alert rule that fired wrongly and a good alert the client simply turned down. That difference didn't exist before, and it is the thing the alert programme had been missing for years.

Design system and delivery

The shared design system cut inconsistencies by 45% and sped up delivery by 35%. Detailed Figma prototypes, flows, and interaction specs made the build smoother and cut design rework by 28%.

"Advisors are faster, which we expected. What we didn't expect is that the list now tells us which of our own alerts are worth sending. We have wanted that for years."

— Product lead, internal release retro
09
What's Next

Next: turning those reasons into better alerts

The redesign made the list produce data. Nothing reads that data yet. The closing reasons land in a report that someone has to go and open, so the loop still runs on a person noticing. An alert rule that keeps firing wrongly will keep firing until somebody spots the pattern.

My theory: send those reasons back to the team that owns the alert rules, as a ranked list of which rules produce the most “should never have fired” closures. That should cut pointless alerts without any advisor filing a ticket. Success here isn't a screen metric. It is whether the list gets shorter on its own.

The tracking has been in place since the first release, which is the argument for doing this next. The data is piling up whether we use it or not, and the longer it piles up the better the first round of fixes will be. The open question is where it should live: a report the rules team goes and opens, or something that reaches them without being asked.

Constraints

A regulated bank, a live tool, and two releases to work with

This is a bank, so the usual consumer app freedoms don't apply. Every decision had to pass three tests before shipping: compliance, whether the record would stand up to an audit, and the fact that advisors were using the tool for real the whole time we were changing it. Time wasn't the real limit. How much I could change inside two releases was.

I protected scope and quality. Time was what I let move.

Compliance reviews take as long as they take, and the list of closing options needed sign-off because it changes what the firm's records say. So the thing I let slip was the schedule. The work shipped across two releases instead of one: the list, panel, and closing options first, the dashboard second. Cutting scope was risky, because new options without the panel would have added work with no speed gain, and that is how a rollout gets rejected. Cutting quality wasn't an option in a regulated business. So the timeline moved. That was the right call.

01
Researching Without Touching Client Data
Normal usability testing wasn't possible, because the work is full of confidential client information. So I worked around it. When sitting with advisors I recorded how they moved through the tool, never what was on screen. Concept tests used made-up accounts. The diary study asked advisors to describe their reasoning, not the case. Four limited methods together gave a clearer picture than one open one would have.
02
What Moved to The Next Cycle
A way to send closing reasons back to the rules team. Setting the status on many related alerts at once. Saved filter sets, which advisors asked for again and again and we chose to leave out rather than build badly. All written up with owners and success measures, and all already being tracked, so the next sprint starts with a brief instead of a blank page.
03
How AI Tooling Compressed Design Cycles
Figma Make sped up building the component variations. The row alone needed a version for every status, owner, and note state. Claude Code let me prototype how the panel opens, steps, and collapses, so I could test the lost-your-place problem before engineering committed to anything. Together that shipped a compliance-heavy tool in two releases instead of three.

"In a regulated business, what limits you is how much you can change before the review process starts again. So you pick fewer changes and you make each one count."

11
Reflection

What I took from this project

01
A Queue Can Measure Itself
The most valuable thing here wasn't speed. It was making everyday work produce information. Any job done thousands of times is a place where thousands of human judgements get made and thrown away. Catching them costs almost nothing while someone is deciding, and can't be recovered afterwards.
02
Interrogate The Obvious Brief
The brief said the problem was too many alerts, and the obvious fix was fewer alerts. The evidence said the real limit was what it cost to check one. Volume was a symptom. If I had built what the brief asked for, we would have ended up with a shorter list that still taught the bank nothing.
03
Design The Audit Trail
Every regulated screen I design now starts with a different question. Not “how do we show this data” but “how would someone explain this decision a year from now”. That one change reorganises everything: how specific the options are, how notes are stored, whose name goes on what.
04
Make Research A Shared Verdict
The product managers knew this business deeply and held views the data hadn't tested yet. Instead of arguing about conclusions, I argued for the method, and brought them into the sessions. When a result went against what they expected, it landed as something we found together rather than a designer overruling the people who own the product.
05
Engineering Limits Are Design Inputs
The chart tool's defaults were built to draw charts quickly, not to make them clear. Treating that as something to design with, and sizing the changes with engineers before they reached the build plan, got better charts shipped than either fighting the tool or quietly accepting it.
06
Working A Sprint Ahead
Working a sprint ahead of 15 engineers meant settling the awkward cases before the sprint opened, not during it. Making those decisions early, and lining up compliance, accessibility, and the rules team at the same time, changed how I plan design work more than anything else on this project.

Conclusion

Make the everyday decision cheaper and people make more of them. Have it record a reason, and the business finally learns from work it was already doing.

The single list, side panel, closing options that record a reason, and shared design system shipped at Morgan Stanley are now the platform standard — driving continued improvements in advisor productivity and platform adoption.
